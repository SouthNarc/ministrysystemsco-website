How to Get Soundboard Audio Into a Church Livestream

By Ministry Systems Co. · October 12, 2026

Your church sounds good in the sanctuary.

The pastor’s microphone is clear.

The worship team is balanced.

The congregation can hear everything.

But when you watch the livestream later, something is wrong.

The pastor sounds distant.

The music sounds thin.

Maybe the stream is using the camera microphone instead of the church sound system.

This is one of the most common problems in church livestreaming, and the solution usually begins with one basic idea:

Your livestream needs a clean audio feed from the soundboard.

Getting that signal into the streaming computer may seem complicated at first, but the basic signal path is actually fairly simple.

The Basic Audio Signal Path

A typical church livestream audio system looks something like this:

Microphones → Soundboard → Livestream Output → Audio Interface → Streaming Computer → OBS → Internet

Every part of that chain has a job.

The microphones capture the sound.

The soundboard mixes it.

A dedicated output sends that mix toward the livestream.

An audio interface converts the signal into something the computer can use.

OBS receives the audio and sends it with the video.

Understanding that path makes troubleshooting much easier.

Why You Should Not Rely on the Camera Microphone

Most cameras include a built-in microphone.

That does not mean it should be the primary audio source for your livestream.

A camera microphone sitting at the back of a sanctuary hears the room very differently than a person sitting in the congregation.

It may capture:

  • room echo
  • HVAC noise
  • people talking nearby
  • chairs moving
  • excessive congregation noise
  • weak speech from the platform

The result often sounds distant and hollow.

Your soundboard already has access to the microphones being used during worship.

That makes it a much better starting point.

Do Not Assume the Main House Mix Will Sound Good Online

There is another common mistake.

A church sends the exact same mix used for the sanctuary directly to the livestream.

Sometimes that works reasonably well.

Often it does not.

The sanctuary and the livestream have different needs.

Imagine a drummer playing loudly in the room.

The sound engineer may barely need that drum kit in the house speakers because the congregation can already hear it acoustically.

But the person watching online does not hear the room directly.

They hear only what is being sent into the stream.

If the drums are missing from that mix, the online viewer may barely hear them.

The same thing can happen with:

  • guitar amplifiers
  • acoustic piano
  • choir
  • congregation
  • drums
  • other instruments that are naturally loud in the sanctuary

That is why a dedicated livestream mix can be so valuable.

The Best Option: A Dedicated AUX or Matrix Mix

If your soundboard supports it, one of the best approaches is creating a separate output specifically for the livestream.

Depending on the mixer, this may be called an:

  • AUX
  • bus
  • matrix
  • mix
  • output

The exact terminology varies.

The principle is the same.

You create a mix that is independent enough from the sanctuary mix to serve the online audience.

That allows you to adjust livestream levels without drastically changing what people hear in the room.

For example, the worship leader may need less volume in the sanctuary but more volume online.

A separate livestream mix lets you make that adjustment.

What If Your Mixer Does Not Support a Separate Mix?

Not every church has a large digital soundboard.

That does not automatically mean you need to replace it.

Depending on your equipment, you may be able to use:

  • an auxiliary output
  • record output
  • subgroup output
  • matrix output
  • main output split
  • USB audio directly from the mixer

The goal is to obtain a clean line-level feed that can be sent to the streaming system.

The exact solution depends on the mixer.

But there is an important principle:

Use an output designed for audio equipment rather than improvising with the wrong connector or signal level.

Do You Need an Audio Interface?

Often, yes.

An audio interface sits between the soundboard and the computer.

Its job is to take the analog audio coming from the mixer and convert it into digital audio the computer can use.

A typical path might look like:

Soundboard AUX Output → Audio Interface → USB → Streaming Computer

OBS then sees the interface as an audio device.

This is usually cleaner and more reliable than trying to feed professional audio directly into a computer’s microphone input.

Some Mixers Can Connect Directly by USB

Many digital mixers include built-in USB audio.

In that case, you may not need a separate audio interface.

The mixer itself can act as the interface.

The path becomes:

Soundboard → USB → Streaming Computer → OBS

That can simplify the system significantly.

However, you still need to verify exactly which mixer channels or buses are being sent over USB.

A USB connection does not automatically guarantee that OBS is receiving the mix you intended.

Mic Level and Line Level Are Not the Same

This is an important concept.

Microphones produce relatively weak signals.

Soundboards generally produce much stronger line-level signals from their outputs.

If you send a strong line-level signal into an input expecting microphone level, you may get:

  • distortion
  • clipping
  • poor audio
  • extremely sensitive level controls

Make sure the receiving device is configured for the type of signal the mixer is providing.

This is one reason proper audio interfaces are useful.

They are designed to work with professional audio signals.

Balanced Connections Are Usually Better

For longer cable runs, balanced audio connections are generally preferable.

Common balanced connections include:

  • XLR
  • TRS

Balanced connections are designed to reject electrical noise picked up along the cable.

That can help reduce:

  • hum
  • buzz
  • interference

This matters especially when the soundboard and livestream computer are separated by some distance.

Set the Soundboard Level First

A common mistake is trying to fix everything inside OBS.

OBS can adjust volume.

But it cannot repair a badly distorted signal coming from the soundboard.

You want a healthy signal before it reaches the computer.

The general principle is:

Good source level → good mixer level → good interface level → good OBS level

This is called gain structure.

Each stage should receive an appropriate signal.

If one stage is too low, you may introduce noise when you try to compensate later.

If one stage is too high, you may introduce distortion.

Watch the OBS Audio Meter

Once the signal reaches OBS, you should see movement on the appropriate audio meter.

That confirms something is reaching the software.

But the meter alone does not tell you whether the mix sounds good.

You still need to listen.

A meter can tell you:

There is audio.

Your ears tell you:

This audio is usable.

You need both.

Avoid Clipping

Digital clipping happens when the audio signal becomes too loud.

In OBS, the audio meters help show how close the signal is to the maximum level.

You do not want the livestream audio constantly hitting the top of the meter.

Speech should be strong and easy to hear without repeatedly clipping.

Leave some headroom for louder moments.

A pastor may suddenly raise their voice.

The worship leader may sing louder.

The congregation may respond.

The system needs enough margin to handle those moments.

Speech Should Be the Priority

If viewers cannot understand the sermon, the livestream has failed at its most basic job.

That means speech should usually receive first priority.

Before worrying about whether the cymbals sound perfect, make sure:

  • the pastor is clear
  • Scripture readers can be heard
  • prayer is understandable
  • announcements are audible

Music matters.

But intelligible speech matters more.

Be Careful With Double Audio

One of the most recognizable church livestream audio problems is echo.

This often happens because OBS is receiving the same sound from two places.

For example:

Soundboard audio + camera microphone audio

The camera microphone hears the sanctuary slightly later than the direct soundboard signal.

When those two signals play together, the result can sound:

  • hollow
  • doubled
  • echoey
  • distant

If the camera microphone is not intentionally part of the mix, mute it.

Know exactly which audio sources are supposed to be active in OBS.

Add Congregation Sound Carefully

A direct soundboard mix can sometimes sound unnaturally dry.

The online viewer hears microphones and instruments but very little sense of the room.

Carefully placed ambient microphones can help.

They may capture:

  • congregational singing
  • applause
  • responses
  • the natural atmosphere of worship

But room microphones should be used carefully.

Too much room sound brings back the echo you were trying to avoid.

The goal is usually:

clarity first, atmosphere second.

Watch for Audio/Video Sync

Audio and video sometimes travel through different processing paths.

That can cause the picture and sound to arrive at slightly different times.

You may notice that someone's lips do not match their voice.

A simple test is to stand on camera and clap.

Watch the recording.

Does the sound of the clap occur at the same moment the hands meet?

If not, OBS allows you to compensate for timing differences.

Do not guess.

Record, test, and adjust.

Use Headphones During Setup

Headphones are invaluable when setting up livestream audio.

They allow you to hear problems that may be hidden by the sound coming from the sanctuary speakers.

Listen for:

  • hum
  • buzz
  • distortion
  • missing microphones
  • excessive room sound
  • uneven music
  • echo
  • low speech volume

What sounds fine in the room may sound completely different in headphones.

Test the Actual Stream

This is one of the most important steps.

Do not stop after listening inside OBS.

Open the actual livestream on another device.

Use:

  • a phone
  • tablet
  • laptop
  • television

Listen as a viewer would.

That tests the full path:

Soundboard → Interface → Computer → OBS → Internet → Streaming Platform → Viewer

If you only test at the mixer, you have not tested the livestream.

Test on More Than One Device

People experience livestream audio through very different speakers.

A mix that sounds excellent through studio headphones may not work as well through a phone.

Try listening on:

  • headphones
  • smartphone speaker
  • laptop
  • television

Ask:

Can I understand every word?

That question matters more than whether the mix sounds impressive through expensive headphones.

Build a Repeatable Sunday Check

Audio should be part of the Sunday startup procedure.

A simple check might be:

  1. Turn on the sound system.
  2. Confirm the livestream output.
  3. Check the audio interface.
  4. Open OBS.
  5. Confirm the correct audio source.
  6. Speak into the pastor's microphone.
  7. Watch the OBS meter.
  8. Listen with headphones.
  9. Start a test stream.
  10. Verify the actual stream on another device.

That process can prevent a large number of Sunday problems.

Label the Audio Path

Do not make volunteers guess which cable carries livestream audio.

Label it.

For example:

Soundboard STREAM AUX → Audio Interface Input 1

Then document the same path in the Sunday runbook.

That makes troubleshooting far easier.

If There Is No Audio, Follow the Signal

Do not randomly click settings.

Start where the sound originates.

Is the microphone working?

If yes:

Is it reaching the mixer?

If yes:

Is the livestream bus receiving it?

If yes:

Is the signal leaving the mixer?

If yes:

Is the audio interface receiving it?

If yes:

Does the computer see the interface?

If yes:

Does OBS have the correct source selected?

This leads to one of the most useful troubleshooting questions in church technology:

Where was the last place the signal was known to be working?

Start there.

A Simple Church Livestream Audio System Is Often the Best One

It is possible to build extremely sophisticated broadcast audio systems.

Most small churches do not need one.

They need:

  • clear speech
  • balanced music
  • dependable connections
  • understandable controls
  • documented signal flow
  • volunteers who know what to check

That is enough to dramatically improve the viewer experience.

The Eyes-Closed Test

Here is one of my favorite ways to evaluate church livestream audio.

Play five minutes of the livestream.

Close your eyes.

Do not look at the video.

Ask:

  • Can I understand the pastor easily?
  • Does the music sound balanced?
  • Can I tell that a congregation is present?
  • Is anything distracting me?
  • Would I keep listening?

If the answer is yes, the audio is probably doing its job.

If not, another camera will not solve the problem.

Start with the sound.

The Goal Is Clarity

Church livestream audio does not have to sound like a professionally produced album.

It does need to communicate clearly.

The person watching from home should be able to hear the Word being preached, join in worship, and remain engaged without fighting the technology.

That starts by getting a clean signal from the soundboard into the livestream.

Build the path.

Document it.

Test it.

Train someone else to operate it.

That is how church technology becomes:

Reliable. Repeatable. Volunteer-Friendly.
