Why Does OBS Keep Dropping Frames During Church Livestreams?

By Ministry Systems Co. · September 29, 2026

You are ten minutes into the service.

Everything looked fine during setup.

The camera is working. The audio meters are moving. OBS says the stream is live.

Then the warning appears:

Dropped frames detected.

The video begins stuttering.

The stream buffers.

Maybe viewers start sending messages saying the livestream is freezing.

For a church volunteer, that is one of the most stressful problems to see on Sunday morning.

The good news is that dropped frames usually point to a fairly specific kind of problem.

And the first step is understanding what OBS actually means when it says frames are being dropped.

What Are Dropped Frames in OBS?

In simple terms, OBS creates video frames and sends them to the streaming platform.

When OBS reports dropped frames, it usually means some of those frames could not be successfully sent over the network.

That is different from a computer that cannot render or encode video fast enough.

The distinction matters because the solution depends on where the problem is happening.

There are three broad categories of livestream trouble:

  • network problems
  • encoding problems
  • rendering problems

They can look similar to the viewer, but they are not the same problem.

Dropped Frames Usually Point to the Network

When OBS specifically reports dropped frames related to the connection, the first place to investigate is the network path between the streaming computer and the internet.

That path may include:

Streaming Computer → Ethernet Cable → Network Switch → Router → Internet Provider → Streaming Platform

A problem anywhere along that path can interfere with the stream.

Possible causes include:

  • insufficient upload bandwidth
  • unstable internet service
  • Wi-Fi interference
  • network congestion
  • a bad Ethernet cable
  • a failing switch port
  • an overloaded router
  • other devices consuming upload bandwidth
  • temporary ISP problems
  • problems reaching the streaming platform

The important thing is not to assume the internet plan itself is the only cause.

First Question: Is the Streaming Computer on Wi-Fi?

If the answer is yes, that should be one of the first things you investigate.

Wi-Fi is convenient.

It is not always predictable.

A church building can create difficult wireless conditions because of:

  • thick walls
  • brick
  • concrete
  • metal
  • multiple rooms
  • long distances
  • crowded wireless channels
  • large numbers of connected devices

A Wi-Fi connection that seems fine during the week may become unstable when the building fills on Sunday.

For critical livestream equipment, wired Ethernet is usually the better choice when practical.

That does not mean Wi-Fi never works.

It means the livestream should not depend on wireless if a reliable wired connection is available.

Upload Speed Matters More Than Download Speed

Many churches know the advertised speed of their internet plan.

The problem is that they often focus on the wrong number.

For livestreaming, upload capacity matters. Our church livestream internet speed guide explains how to plan for steady upload speed and bandwidth headroom.

Your stream has to send video out of the building.

If the church has strong download speed but weak upload speed, the livestream may struggle.

This becomes even more important when other devices are also uploading data.

Examples include:

  • cloud backups
  • security cameras
  • phones syncing photos
  • staff computers
  • online storage
  • video calls
  • other livestreams

The internet connection may be fast enough in theory but overloaded in practice.

Test During Real Sunday Conditions

A speed test on Tuesday afternoon is not enough.

The church may have only a few connected devices at that time.

Sunday is different.

People arrive.

Phones connect.

Staff systems come online.

Presentation equipment is running.

The livestream starts.

The real question is not:

How fast is our internet when the building is empty?

The better question is:

How stable is our connection when the church is operating normally?

That is why testing under real load matters.

Bitrate Can Be Too High

A common mistake is setting the livestream bitrate based on what seems possible rather than what is reliably sustainable.

A higher bitrate can improve image quality.

It can also make the stream less tolerant of connection problems.

If the available upload bandwidth drops below what OBS is trying to send, frames may be lost.

The right bitrate is not necessarily the highest one your internet connection can hit during a speed test.

It is the bitrate the network can maintain consistently.

Reliability matters more than squeezing out a little more image quality.

Leave Headroom

Do not design the stream to consume nearly all available upload bandwidth.

The network needs breathing room.

Other systems may use bandwidth unexpectedly.

Internet performance also fluctuates.

A church that tries to run the livestream right at the edge of its available connection leaves almost no margin for changing conditions.

A little headroom can make a large difference in stability.

Guest Wi-Fi Can Affect the Livestream

This is one of the reasons church network design matters.

If the livestream system and hundreds of guest devices are all competing for the same network resources, Sunday morning may become unpredictable.

Phones may be:

  • downloading updates
  • watching video
  • syncing files
  • uploading photos
  • refreshing social media

That traffic may not seem significant one device at a time.

Multiply it across a building full of people and it can become meaningful.

This is why it can be helpful to separate:

  • staff traffic
  • guest traffic
  • production traffic

The purpose is not to make the network unnecessarily complicated.

The purpose is to protect critical ministry systems from avoidable competition.

Check the Entire Network Path

If OBS begins dropping frames, do not immediately start changing random settings.

Work through the path logically.

Start with the streaming computer.

Is the Ethernet cable connected securely?

Then check the switch.

Does the port appear healthy?

Then check the router.

Is the internet connection stable?

Then check the ISP.

Is there an outage or degradation?

Then consider whether the problem is only affecting the stream or the entire network.

The most useful troubleshooting question is often:

Where was the last place the connection was known to be working correctly?

That helps narrow the problem.

A Bad Cable Can Cause a Big Problem

Ethernet cables are easy to overlook.

They bend.

They get stepped on.

They are unplugged and reconnected.

They are routed under furniture.

They are sometimes poorly terminated.

If a livestream is experiencing intermittent problems, a cable replacement can be one of the easiest things to test.

The same is true for switch ports.

Sometimes moving the connection to a known-good port can help identify whether the hardware path is part of the problem.

Restarting Everything Is Not a Troubleshooting Strategy

Restarting equipment can temporarily fix a problem.

But it does not necessarily tell you what caused it.

If the church has to reboot the router every Sunday, the real problem has not been solved.

A good troubleshooting process should identify whether the issue is:

  • bandwidth
  • congestion
  • hardware
  • cabling
  • Wi-Fi
  • configuration
  • ISP instability
  • platform connectivity

Otherwise, the church may repeat the same emergency every week.

Dropped Frames Are Not the Same as Encoding Lag

This distinction is important.

If OBS reports dropped frames because of the network, the computer may be perfectly capable of creating the video.

The problem is getting that video to the internet.

If OBS reports encoding overload, the problem is different.

That may involve:

  • CPU load
  • GPU load
  • encoder settings
  • too many sources
  • high resolution
  • high frame rate
  • heavy filters
  • other programs using system resources

Network troubleshooting will not fix an overloaded encoder.

Likewise, buying a faster computer will not solve an unstable internet connection.

Make sure you are solving the right problem.

Rendering Lag Is Different Too

Rendering problems happen before encoding.

They may involve:

  • GPU load
  • complex scenes
  • too many visual sources
  • high-resolution assets
  • demanding browser sources
  • other graphics-heavy applications

Again, the viewer may simply see stuttering video.

But OBS may be telling you whether the issue is rendering, encoding, or network delivery.

Pay attention to the specific warning. For a simpler volunteer workflow, see our OBS for Churches guide.

Simplify Before You Upgrade

When a church sees dropped frames, it is tempting to immediately buy:

  • a new computer
  • a new router
  • faster internet
  • a new switch

Sometimes new equipment is needed.

But troubleshooting should come first.

Check:

  • connection type
  • upload stability
  • bitrate
  • network load
  • cabling
  • switch port
  • OBS statistics
  • other devices using bandwidth

You may discover that the existing equipment is adequate but the system needs to be configured differently.

Build a Sunday Troubleshooting Sequence

A simple church troubleshooting flow might be:

  1. Confirm OBS is reporting network dropped frames
  2. Confirm the streaming computer is wired
  3. Check the Ethernet cable
  4. Check the switch connection
  5. Test internet connectivity
  6. Check upload performance
  7. Look for unusually heavy network activity
  8. Reduce bitrate if necessary
  9. Move to the simplest reliable stream configuration
  10. Contact the ISP or tech lead if the problem continues

The volunteer does not need to understand every networking concept.

They need a logical sequence.

Have a Fallback Plan

Sometimes the internet problem cannot be fixed during worship.

The church still needs a plan.

That might mean:

  • continuing to record locally
  • reducing livestream quality
  • switching to a backup connection
  • using a cellular hotspot
  • uploading the recording later

The goal during worship is not always to fully repair the network.

Sometimes the right decision is to preserve the service and deal with the technical problem afterward.

Document What Happened

After the service, write down:

  • when the problem started
  • what OBS reported
  • current bitrate
  • upload speed
  • whether Wi-Fi or Ethernet was used
  • whether other devices were affected
  • what was changed
  • whether the problem stopped

That turns a frustrating Sunday into useful information.

Over time, patterns may become obvious.

The Bigger Issue Is Reliability

Dropped frames are often treated as an OBS problem.

Sometimes they are really a network-design problem.

A church livestream depends on more than software.

It depends on:

  • a stable computer
  • reliable audio
  • dependable cabling
  • a healthy network
  • adequate internet service
  • good documentation
  • trained volunteers

OBS sits in the middle of all of that.

The goal is not simply to make the warning disappear.

The goal is to build a system that works consistently enough that volunteers do not have to panic every Sunday.
